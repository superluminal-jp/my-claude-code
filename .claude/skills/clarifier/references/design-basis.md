# Design basis — theory that governs this capability's own behaviour

This layer explains *why* the procedure is shaped the way it is. It is **not** named to the
user. Naming these would turn the exchange into a lecture about the user rather than a
judgement about the work.

One exception, and only one: **Group F may be named as the reason for the AI's own behaviour**
— never as a claim about the user's mind. Groups E and G are never named at all.

## Group E — Design of the learning path

*Governs: attaching a framework name to the judgement it produced, and never explaining the
framework on its own.*

| Theory | Source |
|---|---|
| Modelling in cognitive apprenticeship | Collins, Brown & Newman, "Cognitive Apprenticeship: Teaching the Crafts of Reading, Writing, and Mathematics," in Resnick (Ed.), *Knowing, Learning, and Instruction: Essays in Honor of Robert Glaser*, Erlbaum, 1989, pp. 453–494 |
| Worked-example effect | Sweller & Cooper, "The Use of Worked Examples as a Substitute for Problem Solving in Learning Algebra," *Cognition and Instruction* 2(1): 59–89, 1985 |
| Scaffolding and fading | Wood, Bruner & Ross, "The Role of Tutoring in Problem Solving," *Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry* 17(2): 89–100, 1976 |
| Legitimate peripheral participation | Lave & Wenger, *Situated Learning*, Cambridge University Press, 1991 |

Why the name is never explained on its own: the user is here to get work done, not to take a
course. A name beside a concrete judgement is a worked example; a name with a paragraph of
exposition is an interruption. The vocabulary is acquired as a by-product of the work, without
the user spending time on it.

Naming this group would announce that the user is being taught — which changes the relationship
the user asked for.

## Group F — Why restatement is necessary

*Governs: stating the AI's understanding explicitly instead of assuming it was transmitted.*

| Theory | Source |
|---|---|
| Curse of knowledge | Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber, "The Curse of Knowledge in Economic Settings," *Journal of Political Economy* 97(5): 1232–1254, 1989; Newton, *The Rocky Road from Actions to Intentions*, Stanford University doctoral dissertation, 1990 (tappers predicted ~50% recognition; observed 3/120 = 2.5%) |
| Illusion of transparency | Gilovich, Savitsky & Medvec, "The Illusion of Transparency: Biased Assessments of Others' Ability to Read One's Emotional States," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 75(2): 332–346, 1998 |
| False consensus effect | Ross, Greene & House, "The False Consensus Effect," *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* 13(3): 279–301, 1977 |

These biases run in **both** directions. That symmetry is exactly what licenses the exception:
the AI may cite one as a reason for its own restatement, because the bias applies to itself
just as much.

- Permitted: "I'll restate my reading rather than assume it carried across — the illusion of
  transparency cuts both ways."
- Forbidden: "You're suffering from the curse of knowledge, so your explanation is incomplete."
  This asserts a fact about the user's mind that cannot be observed, and shifts blame for a
  failure of grounding onto them.

## Group G — Theory of coordination

*Governs: not starting the work until the understanding is corrected or approved, and bounding
the exchange.*

| Theory | Source |
|---|---|
| Cooperative principle and maxims | Grice, "Logic and Conversation," in Cole & Morgan (Eds.), *Syntax and Semantics 3: Speech Acts*, Academic Press, 1975 |
| Conversation for action | Winograd & Flores, *Understanding Computers and Cognition*, Ablex, 1986 |
| Contribution model and grounding cost | Clark & Brennan, "Grounding in Communication," in Resnick, Levine & Teasley (Eds.), *Perspectives on Socially Shared Cognition*, APA, 1991, Ch. 7, pp. 127–149 |

Grounding has a cost, and the contribution model says the participants minimise the *joint*
cost, not either side's alone. That is the argument for a bounded number of exchanges: past a
few rounds, further confirmation costs more than the residual uncertainty it removes. It is
also why these are never named to the user — they generate no criterion about the user's work,
only an account of the procedure, which would be exposition rather than judgement.
